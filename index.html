Beyond the Balance

So, what actually is super?

Superannuation, usually just called super, is money put aside during your working life to help pay for life after work. In Australia, employers generally have to contribute a percentage of eligible employees’ earnings into super, where the money is invested over time. It’s your money for the future, but it can be hard to know why you need it, who has it, how much you need and why are there so many different funds?

The most common words — superannuation, fund, super and scheme — reflect the industry's focus on managing and organising retirement savings. But the names also reveal how the sector presents itself to members. Words like Australian, trust, retirement, service, wealth and first evoke national identity, reliability, security and a commitment to members' financial futures. It shows that superannuation is not just about managing money; it is also about building confidence in a system designed to support Australians in retirement.

Preparing for an ageing Australia

Australia's ageing population makes retirement planning increasingly important. But the retirement landscape is not uniform: retirees are concentrated in some areas more than others, creating different local needs and highlighting the importance of preparing financially for later life.

Australia’s superannuation landscape reflects both where people live and the opportunities they have had to accumulate retirement savings. New South Wales and Victoria stand out in the eastern states, while Queensland also has substantial coastal and peri-regional communities with older populations. The relationship between age and super is not straightforward. Older working-age Australians have generally had more time to make contributions and build balances, while retirees may draw down their savings over time. Areas with older populations can therefore have different superannuation profiles, but age alone does not tell the whole story.

Who Holds Australia’s Super?

Superannuation is one of Australia’s most significant forms of household wealth, but it is not distributed evenly. Balances vary across age groups, genders and states, reflecting differences in earnings, employment patterns, time spent in the workforce and contribution histories. Looking beyond total balances reveals a more detailed picture of retirement savings: how balances change over the life course, where gender differences emerge, how average balances vary between states, and how many superannuation accounts people hold. Together, these measures help explain not just how much super Australia holds, but how that wealth is distributed.

Men consistently hold higher superannuation benefits and average account balances across age groups, highlighting persistent gender differences in retirement savings. The disparity in average balances is particularly pronounced between ages 35 and 39, when men gain an average balance advantage of $11,771. This period coincides with the years when many women experience career interruptions or reduced working hours associated with maternity leave and caring responsibilities, which may reduce their superannuation contributions. The gap narrows from age 60 onwards, as people transition into retirement and begin drawing down their superannuation. Women's balances may decline more gradually during this period, potentially reflecting differences in retirement timing, withdrawal patterns and longevity.

The Funds Behind Retirement

Superannuation outcomes depend not only on how much Australians contribute, but also on where their money is invested. Differences in fund membership, investment performance and fees can shape retirement balances over time.
